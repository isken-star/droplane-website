The ATO car logbook: 12 weeks, 5,000 km and 91c a km

The two ways to claim your own car for work, the 2026–27 rate, when a logbook beats cents per km, and what the ATO wants written in it.

If you use your own car for work, the ATO gives you two ways to claim it: a flat rate per kilometre, or the logbook method, where you claim a share of what the car actually cost. Both work for employees and sole traders. Which one gives you more depends on how far you drive and what the car costs. This guide covers the 2026–27 income year, from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027.

The cents per km rate

Income yearRateLimit
2026–2791c per km5,000 work km per car
2025–26 (the return you've just lodged)88c per km5,000 work km per car

That's a maximum claim of $4,550 per car for 2026–27. The rate covers everything: fuel, rego, insurance, servicing and the car losing value. You can't claim any of those on top. You don't need a logbook for this method, but you do need to be able to show how you worked out your work kilometres, for example from a diary of trips.

The logbook method

With the logbook method there's no 5,000 km limit. You add up what the car cost you for the year and claim the work-use share. The logbook is how you prove that share.

A car costs $12,000 to run for the year: fuel, rego, insurance, servicing, tyres and the decline in value.

The 12-week logbook shows 40% of the kilometres were for work.

Logbook claim: $12,000 × 40% = $4,800

Cents per km, at most: 5,000 km × 91c = $4,550

In this example the logbook wins, and the gap grows quickly. At 60% work use the same car gives a $7,200 claim, while cents per km stays capped at $4,550. If you drive well over 5,000 work kilometres a year, or the car is expensive to run, the logbook is usually the one to keep.

What counts as work travel

  • Driving between two workplaces, or to clients, customers or job sites. Counts.
  • Driving to a place you don't usually work, such as a training course or a different site for the day. Usually counts.
  • Home to your usual workplace and back. That's private travel, even if it's a long drive or you make work calls on the way.
  • Private trips. Don't count, even in the same car on the same day.

These rules are for cars. A ute or van built to carry a tonne or more, a vehicle with nine or more seats, and a motorbike are not cars for this purpose. Those are claimed on actual costs and their work use, not at 91c a km.

What the logbook has to show

The logbook must cover at least 12 continuous weeks that are typical of how you use the car across the year. For each work journey, record:

  • the date the journey started and ended
  • the odometer reading at the start and end
  • the kilometres travelled
  • the reason for the journey

For the 12 weeks as a whole, record the start and end dates, the odometer readings at the start and end, the total kilometres, the work kilometres, and the work-use percentage that results.

One logbook lasts five years. In each later year you rely on it, keep an odometer reading from the start and end of the year (1 July and 30 June). If your work driving changes a lot, for example with a new job, a new round or a new territory, keep a new logbook. Keep the logbook and your receipts for five years from when you lodge. Fuel can be worked out from your odometer records instead of keeping every receipt.
