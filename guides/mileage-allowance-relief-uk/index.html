Paid less than 55p a mile? Claim the gap from HMRC

If you use your own car for work and your employer pays under HMRC's rate, you can get tax relief on the difference. How much, how to claim, and the log HMRC asks for.

Lots of employers pay mileage for your own car at 25p, 30p or 40p a mile, and some pay nothing. HMRC's approved rate for 2026/27 is higher than that, and the difference isn't lost. You can claim tax relief on it. This is called Mileage Allowance Relief, and the claim itself is free.

This guide is for employees: you're on PAYE and you drive your own car or van for the job. If you're self-employed, the rules are different; see mileage allowance for the self-employed.

HMRC's rates for 2026/27

Your own vehiclePer business mile
Car or van, first 10,000 miles in the tax year55p
Car or van, every mile after that25p
Motorbike24p
Bicycle20p

The car and van rate went up from 45p to 55p on 6 April 2026, the first change since 2011. The 2026/27 tax year runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, and the 10,000-mile count starts again every April.

The sum

You drive 6,000 business miles in your own car. Your employer pays 30p a mile.

HMRC's rate: 6,000 × 55p = £3,300

Your employer paid: 6,000 × 30p = £1,800

The gap you can claim relief on: £1,500

The relief is the gap multiplied by your tax rate, not the gap itself. That's £300 back if you pay basic-rate tax (20%), or £600 at the higher rate (40%). If your employer pays nothing at all, the whole £3,300 counts, which is £660 at the basic rate.

You can't get back more tax than you paid that year. And if your employer pays more than HMRC's rate, it works the other way: the extra is taxable, and usually goes through your payslip.

Claiming for past years

You can claim for the current tax year and the four before it. In 2026/27, that means back to 2022/23. Before 6 April 2026 the rate was 45p, so use 45p for those years. With the same 6,000 miles at 30p, each earlier year gives a gap of £900, which is £180 back at the basic rate. Four missed years and this one add up to just over £1,000 for a basic-rate taxpayer.

Which journeys count

  • Visiting a customer, client, patient or site. Counts.
  • Driving between two workplaces during the day. Counts.
  • Going to a temporary place of work, such as a placement, a project or a training course somewhere other than your usual workplace. Usually counts.
  • Home to your normal workplace and back. That's commuting, and it doesn't count, even if you take work calls on the way.
  • Private trips. They don't count, even in the same car on the same day.

The 55p is meant to cover everything the car costs you: fuel, insurance, servicing, tax and wear. You can't claim any of those on top. Parking and tolls on a work journey are separate expenses.

How to claim

  1. Work out your business miles for each tax year you're claiming, and what your employer paid for them. Your payslips or expense claims show the payments.
  2. Claim on GOV.UK. HMRC's online service is at gov.uk/tax-relief-for-employees. If you fill in a Self Assessment return, you have to claim on the return instead.
  3. Send your mileage log. HMRC asks for copies of your mileage logs, including the postcode where every journey started. If you're claiming for more than one job, send a log for each.

For the current year, HMRC usually changes your tax code, so you pay a little less tax every month. For earlier years you get a refund or a code change. You don't need a claims company: they keep a share of what you get back for filling in a form that HMRC provides free.

The log HMRC asks for

For each business journey:

  • the date
  • the start and end postcodes
  • the reason for the journey
  • the business miles

Keep what your employer paid alongside it, because that's the number taken off. A log you add to as you go, with real postcodes, is far easier to stand behind than a total you estimate from memory at the end of the year.
