The IRS mileage log for 2026: what to record, and the two rates

The IRS changed the mileage rate halfway through 2026. Here's what each mile is worth, which miles count, and the log you need if the IRS asks about your deduction.

If you drive your own car for gig work, deliveries, rideshare or your own business, the standard mileage rate is usually the biggest deduction you have. In 2026 it comes with a catch: the IRS raised it in July, so your miles aren't all worth the same. This guide explains how that works, and what the IRS expects your mileage log to show.

The 2026 rates

Business miles drivenPer mile
January 1 – June 30, 202672.5¢
July 1 – December 31, 202676¢

The IRS raised the rate on July 1 because of higher fuel prices (Announcement 2026-11). The increase doesn't go back in time: a mile you drove in March is still worth 72.5¢. That means your log needs dates, not just a year-end total, or you can't split your miles between the two rates.

What it's worth

A delivery driver logs 9,000 business miles in each half of 2026.

January – June: 9,000 × 72.5¢ = $6,525

July – December: 9,000 × 76¢ = $6,840

Mileage deduction for 2026: $13,365

If you're self-employed, that comes off your profit on Schedule C. That reduces both your income tax and your self-employment tax. Self-employment tax is 15.3%, so the deduction saves about $1,900 of it on its own, before any income tax saving. At the 12% bracket, that's roughly $1,600 more.

Who can take it

  • Self-employed, 1099 and gig drivers: yes, on Schedule C. This covers DoorDash, Uber, Lyft, Instacart, Amazon Flex, Spark and the rest, plus freelancers and anyone running their own business.
  • W-2 employees: no, for most people. The deduction for unreimbursed employee expenses was suspended from 2018 and has now been made permanent. If your employer reimburses mileage, though, a proper log is what keeps that money tax-free, which is covered below.

What the log has to show

For every business trip, the IRS wants:

  • the date
  • where you went
  • the business purpose, such as "DoorDash deliveries" or "client meeting, Main St"
  • the miles

It also asks for your total miles for the year, business and personal, because Form 4562 and Schedule C ask what share of the car's use was business. An odometer reading in January and another in December is the simplest way to back that up.

The record has to be kept at or near the time of each trip. A log you make up from memory at tax time is the kind the IRS most often throws out. Keep it for at least three years after you file.

Miles gig drivers miss

The mileage summary from a delivery or rideshare app usually counts only part of what you drove. Depending on how you work, you may also be able to count:

  • driving to a busy zone or a restaurant cluster to wait for orders
  • driving back from the last drop-off
  • miles between two apps when you run both
  • trips for car supplies, or to the bank to deposit cash tips

Driving from home to where you start working can count as commuting. How the IRS treats it depends on whether your home is your main place of business, so ask a tax pro. Either way, record those miles as you go, so the answer can be applied to the whole year.

Standard rate or actual expenses

Instead of the per-mile rate, you can deduct a business share of what the car actually cost you: gas, insurance, repairs and depreciation. You can't claim both. If you want the standard rate, you have to choose it in the first year you use the car for business. Parking and tolls on business trips can be deducted on top of either method.

If your employer reimburses you

An employer reimbursement of up to 76¢ a mile (72.5¢ for miles before July 1) is tax-free under an accountable plan, as long as you give your employer the same log: date, destination, purpose and miles. If you can't back it up, the payments can be treated as taxable wages.
